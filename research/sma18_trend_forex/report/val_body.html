Is the 18-SMA trend edge real, or is it luck?

Entry — close above the 18-period SMA on two consecutive candles → buy at the close of the second. Exit — flat as soon as price touches the SMA18 again. The rule is unchanged from the original hypothesis. What changed is the burden of proof: every number below exists to try to kill this result, not to flatter it.

Before any result: two bugs I found, one of them in my own validation code

You asked me to make sure no bug was faking a result. Two were. Both are fixed, and both are disclosed here rather than quietly patched, because the second one is the more instructive of the two — it was in the code whose entire job was to detect fake results.

  • Bug 1 — intrabar lookahead in the exit rule (in the strategy engine). The exit checked whether bar i's low/high straddled bar i's own SMA. But that SMA value only exists once bar i has closed, and the touch it is testing for happens during bar i. A resting stop sitting in the market during bar i can only be at the SMA level finalized at bar i−1's close. Fixed to use the prior bar's SMA.
    Direction of the error matters: correcting it made results slightly better (GOLD Sharpe +0.60 → +0.63, ETHUSD +0.97 → +1.30, SOLUSD +1.24 → +1.50). The bug was biasing against the strategy, so it was not the source of the edge — but it was still wrong, and everything below is run on the corrected engine.
  • Bug 2 — a biased null model, in my own Markov validation code. The first corrected run returned p = 0.000 for all 30 assets. That is not a great result, it is a red flag: a null model that nothing can fail is a broken null model. The cause was that I generated each synthetic bar's return and its bar shape (where the high and low sit relative to the close) independently. In real markets those are strongly linked — on gold, corr(return, low/close) = −0.47 and corr(return, high/close) = −0.61. Pairing them at random handed up-days the deep lows of down-days, manufacturing SMA touches that cannot physically happen and making the null far too easy to beat.
    Size of the distortion: fixing it moved the synthetic (null) mean Sharpe on GOLD from −0.63 to +0.14, on BTCUSD from −1.15 to −0.10, on NATGAS from −0.88 to −0.14. Every p-value on this page comes from the corrected null, where bar shapes are resampled conditional on the bar's own return bucket. Had I not caught it, this report would have told you the edge was overwhelming on all 30 instruments. It is not.
  • How you can tell the fix worked: the corrected null now produces a spread of p-values — PLATINUM at 0.644, PALLADIUM at 0.160, CORN at 0.118 all fail to clear it. A null that some assets fail is a null that is actually doing work.

Engine correctness suite — run live when this page was built

Two of these test different things and it matters which: test_no_lookahead re-runs the strategy on a truncated series and demands the shared prefix be identical — that catches cross-bar leakage (reading bars that have not happened). It does not catch the bug above, and I verified that: the buggy engine passed it at every cut point. test_exit_uses_prior_bar_sma is the one that catches intrabar leakage, and it fails against the pre-fix code on 16 of 19 exits. test_fast_engine_matches_reference proves the fast array engine used for all 30,000 simulations is numerically identical to the audited reference engine — without it, every p-value below would be measuring a different strategy than the one backtested. (test_real_data_sanity picks the first cached daily file it finds, which is a leftover from an earlier equity run; it is a smoke test on arbitrary OHLC data, not part of this universe.)

The main test · synthetic markets that never existed

Can the rule beat a market built to have no edge in it?

For each instrument I fit a first-order Markov chain to its own daily returns: returns are bucketed into 5 quantile states, a Laplace-smoothed transition matrix is estimated between them, and new price paths are generated by walking that chain and drawing an actual historical return from the landed state's pool. The synthetic series has the same return distribution, same volatility clustering, and same short-run persistence as the real asset — but its structure beyond one step of memory is destroyed. 500 such universes per asset, and the strategy is backtested on every one. The p-value is the fraction of synthetic universes where the rule did at least as well as it did on the real one.

Regime robustness · non-overlapping windows

Does it survive in periods that share no data with each other?

History is cut into six non-overlapping five-year eras and the strategy is re-run from scratch inside each one — no equity, no SMA state, and no information crosses an era boundary. An edge that is one lucky regime dressed up as a strategy shows up here as a single era carrying everything.

Curve-fit check

Is there anything special about the number 18?

If 18 is the only SMA length that pays, the result is an artifact of someone trying lengths until one worked. If performance sits on a broad plateau across neighbouring lengths, the effect belongs to the market, not to the parameter. Every length from 5 to 60 is swept on all 30 instruments.

Mean Sharpe across SMA lengths 5–60

crypto commodity

This is the cleanest anti-curve-fit evidence on the page. SMA-18 is never the best length on any of the 30 instruments, and its median rank is 21st out of 56. If the result were fitted, 18 would sit on a spike. It sits on a plateau — which also means the original hypothesis was not precisely right about 18 so much as approximately right about "a medium-length moving average."

The test most backtests fail

Does it survive being charged to trade?

A round-trip cost is charged on every single trade, swept from 0 to 50 basis points. An edge that dies at 5bps was never an edge — it was the bid/ask spread being counted as profit.

Mean Sharpe vs. round-trip cost

crypto commodity

The two classes separate sharply here, and this is the practical finding of the whole study: commodities do not survive realistic costs — mean Sharpe falls from +0.29 gross to +0.13 at 20bps and goes negative before 50bps. Crypto barely notices (+1.01 → +0.85 at 50bps), because the trades are large enough relative to cost that a half-percent round trip is noise. Whatever the statistical verdict, a commodity implementation of this rule lives or dies on execution quality.

The verdict, stated as carefully as the evidence allows

  • The crypto result is not luck, and that conclusion is robust. All 14 crypto instruments beat the i.i.d. shuffle and 14/14 beat the Markov null, 13 of them at p = 0.000 — meaning not one of 500 synthetic universes matched the real result. It survives Benjamini-Hochberg correction, every testable era, the full parameter plateau, and 50bps of cost.
  • The commodity result is real but much weaker, and five instruments fail outright. 11 of 16 clear the Markov null; PLATINUM (p=0.644), PALLADIUM (0.160), CORN (0.118), COFFEE (0.076) and WHEAT (0.070) do not. Combined with the cost result, the honest reading is that the commodity edge exists statistically but may not exist economically.
  • The most important caveat is the one the crypto result cannot escape: crypto has almost no history. Most of these coins have 2 testable eras, several have fewer. "Positive in every era" means something very different for GOLD (26 years, 5 eras) than for AVAXUSD (~6 years, 2 eras). The Markov test partly compensates — it generates 500 alternative histories rather than relying on the one we got — but it cannot manufacture regimes the asset never lived through. No crypto instrument here has traded through a decade-long bear market, because none has existed for one.
  • Crypto's daily numbers still contain a directional bet no test here removes. The rule is long-only and crypto rose enormously over the sample. The Markov null does control for this — the synthetic series inherit the same drift, and the rule still loses money on them (mean synthetic Sharpe −0.47) — which is genuine evidence the timing adds value beyond the drift. But no buy-and-hold benchmark is computed, so this page does not tell you whether the rule beats simply holding the asset.
  • Thirty simultaneous tests inflate false positives, which is why FDR correction is applied. At p<0.05 across 30 assets you would expect ~1.5 false positives by chance alone. Benjamini-Hochberg at 5% FDR reduces 25 significant to 22. That correction is already reflected in the headline tile.
  • The Markov null is first-order, and that is a real limitation. It preserves one step of memory and volatility clustering, but not longer-horizon structure, regime switching, or cross-asset correlation. A higher-order or regime-switching null would be a harder test, and some of the marginal commodities (COPPER at 0.048, NATGAS at 0.040, BRENT at 0.032) would likely not survive it. Treat p-values near 0.05 as unresolved rather than as passes.
  • Assets are not independent tests. BRENT and CRUDE are nearly the same instrument; so are GOLD and SILVER, and most large-cap crypto trades as one correlated bloc. The effective number of independent experiments here is far below 30, which means even the FDR-corrected count is optimistic.
  • Costs are modeled as a flat per-trade haircut, not as realistic execution. No slippage, no market impact, no funding, no borrow. The "touch" exit fills exactly at the SMA, which assumes a resting order always gets hit at its price — in a fast move it would not.
  • Nothing here is out-of-sample in the strict sense. The era splits and the Markov test are the closest substitutes, and they are good ones, but the rule was specified in advance by you rather than discovered by search, which is what makes that acceptable. Had I searched for this rule in this data, none of these p-values would mean anything.
