What Monte Carlo actually is, and what it can and cannot tell you

Three different Monte Carlos, answering three different questions

A Monte Carlo simulation means: run the thing thousands of times with the randomness put back in, and look at the whole distribution of outcomes instead of the single one that happened. A backtest gives you one history. That number is a sample of size one. Monte Carlo turns it into a distribution you can ask questions of — but what you randomize decides what question you asked, and the three methods on this page are not interchangeable.

The three methods, in plain terms

  • 1. Trade-order bootstrap (the fan charts further down) — "did I just get lucky with the order?" Take the trades that actually happened, shuffle and resample them 2,000 times, replay each. The set of wins and losses is fixed; only the sequence changes. This is the weakest of the three, because it can never produce a trade the strategy didn't already make. It answers a narrow question: how much of the final number came down to the luck of ordering.
  • 2. Markov synthetic markets (the histograms at the top) — "is there an edge at all?" Here the market itself is randomized, not the trades. A fresh price series is generated with the same return distribution, volatility clustering and one-step memory as the real asset, then the strategy is run on it from scratch, generating its own signals and its own trades. This is the strong test, and it is the one that produced the p-values — because a rule with no edge will still make money on a series that drifts upward, and only this method controls for that.
  • 3. Forward projection (the section below) — "what might the next five years look like?" Draw future trades from the historical distribution and compound them forward 10,000 times. This does not test whether the edge is real — it assumes it is, and asks what follows. Its entire value is in the spread: the gap between the 5th and 95th percentile is the honest width of the uncertainty, and the drawdown percentiles tell you what you would have to sit through.
  • The trap to avoid: a Monte Carlo cannot generate a risk that is absent from its input. None of these can produce an exchange failure, a regime that never occurred in the sample, or the rule simply ceasing to work. A projection that says "88% chance of profit" means 88% conditional on the future resembling the past — which is the assumption actually doing all the work, and it is not tested by the simulation.

Reading these projections honestly

  • The crypto five-year medians are not forecasts and should not be read as any. A median terminal multiple above 15× is the arithmetic of feeding a decade-long bull market's trade distribution into a compounding engine. It tells you what the past distribution implies, which is precisely why it is more useful as a warning about the input than as a projection of the output.
  • An expectation of mine failed here, and the number is left in. I ran a block bootstrap alongside the standard independent-draw one expecting it to reveal deeper drawdowns, on the theory that losing trades cluster and independent draws break the clusters up. It came out 1.6 percentage points shallower (67.0% vs 69.5% at the 95th percentile), not deeper. The likely reason is that the touch-the-SMA exit closes a position the moment the trend breaks, so consecutive trades carry little autocorrelation for the block method to preserve. Both numbers are shown because the comparison is the evidence.
  • Drawdowns are measured trade-to-trade, so the real ones are worse. Equity is marked at each trade's close, which misses the dip inside an open trade. Every drawdown figure here is a floor, not a ceiling — and the floors are already brutal: a 69% worst-case five-year drawdown on crypto long-only.
  • Each instrument is projected in isolation. These do not add up to a portfolio, because large-cap crypto trades as one correlated bloc — holding ten of them is far closer to ten units of one bet than to ten independent bets, and a portfolio built this way would carry most of the drawdown of a single name.

Testing an explanation · does liquidity decide where this works?

Is the edge just a liquidity effect?

The proposition: the rule pays on deeply liquid instruments and fails on thin ones. That is a claim about a cross-sectional relationship, so it needs a liquidity number per instrument measured against the result — not an eyeball over the winners. Liquidity is median daily notional USD traded over the last three years, with every futures contract converted using its real multiplier, since gold is 100 troy oz and corn is 5,000 bushels and raw contract counts are not comparable.

A data problem that would have faked a confirmation

  • Yahoo's continuous-futures volume is broken for six of the sixteen commodities. PL=F prints a median of 2 contracts/day with 38% zero-volume days; SI=F prints 87. Real exchange volume for both runs into the tens of thousands. GOLD (186k), CRUDE (296k) and CORN (163k) are plausible and match reality, so the feed is not uniformly wrong — it is wrong in specific places.
  • This mattered more than it looks. PLATINUM and PALLADIUM are precisely the two instruments that failed the Markov test. Ranking them as near-zero liquidity on broken data would have produced a textbook confirmation of the hypothesis being tested — thin markets fail, liquid markets work — built entirely on a data artifact. All six are excluded from the correlations below: SILVER, PLATINUM, PALLADIUM, COPPER, COFFEE, COTTON.
