Does an 18-SMA long trend rule earn its keep on low-vol, trend-sustaining FX pairs?

Long only. Indicator: 18-period SMA of daily close. Entry — close above SMA18 on two consecutive candles → buy at the close of the second. Exit — flat as soon as price touches the SMA18 again. Tested against the pair-selection idea as stated: favor pairs where trend tends to sustain and relative volatility runs slightly lower than peers.

Reading this result honestly

  • Low win rate is expected, not a bug. Win rates sit at 20–33% across every pair — normal for a rule that cuts every pullback to the SMA and only pays off on the trades that run far before mean-reverting back to it. Judge it on Sharpe / profit factor, not win rate.
  • The volatility half of the hypothesis did not hold in this test. Annualized volatility correlates positively with Sharpe across the 14 pairs, not negatively — the opposite of "lower relative volatility does better." The trend-persistence half held up much better (see correlations above the scatter).
  • The strict "fits both filters" bucket is tiny (3 of 14 pairs) and mixed: two of the three (USDJPY, EURJPY) were the best performers in the whole universe, but the third (GBPUSD) had one of the worst Sharpes. A 3-pair bucket isn't enough to call the joint filter validated — the continuous correlations are the more reliable read.
  • No transaction costs, spread, or swap/rollover are modeled. Spot FX spreads and overnight financing on a held long position are real drags this backtest ignores entirely — they would lower every number here, most on the shorter-average-holding-day pairs.
  • "Touch" is filled at the SMA price (or at the open if the candle gaps through it) — i.e. a resting stop exactly at the SMA. Real fills would see slippage, especially around the gap-through case.
  • Single 2015–present sample, no out-of-sample split. The period includes a multi-year USD/JPY rate-differential trend (2022–2024) that likely flatters yen pairs specifically — that's a regime effect, not necessarily a structural property of those pairs.
  • Daily bars only, single asset class. This part tests daily closes on FX only — Part 2 below re-runs the identical rule across four candle sizes and four asset classes.

Monte Carlo — how much of this is luck?

Each chart bootstrap-resamples that instrument/timeframe's own set of realized trade outcomes 2,000 times (with replacement) — same trades, random order/sample — and plots the 5th–95th and 25th–75th percentile bands of the resulting equity paths against the actual realized path (bold line). A wide band or an actual path that hugs the edge means the headline Sharpe is more a function of trade order than of a durable edge; a narrow band with the actual path tracking the median means the result is a more stable property of the trade set.

Reading Part 2 (and the Monte Carlo) honestly

  • 15m and 30m are ~60 calendar days of data — that's a Yahoo/yfinance hard limit, not a choice. 4h (resampled from 1h) covers up to ~2 years; 1d covers 2015–present. A 60-day window is a small, single-regime sample: trust the direction of the 15m/30m results (worse than 4h/1d, consistently), not the exact Sharpe/CAGR magnitude — annualizing a 60-day return blows small differences up a lot (e.g. USDCAD's 15m Sharpe of roughly −27 comes from a real but unremarkable −13.8% raw 60-day return, not a data error).
  • The Monte Carlo bootstrap resamples trade order, not the market. It answers "given this exact multiset of win/loss outcomes, how much does the result depend on the sequence they happened to arrive in" — it does not simulate new price paths or new signals, and it treats trades as independent draws, which understates real uncertainty since consecutive trades on the same instrument share the same regime. Treat the bands as a lower bound on how uncertain the result really is, not an upper bound. Combinations with fewer than 20 trades are excluded from Monte Carlo entirely (too little data to resample meaningfully).
  • The volatility finding reverses direction from Part 1's framing, and it's now the stronger, more consistent result. Within every single timeframe tested, the empirical high-volatility half of the universe beat the low-volatility half on mean Sharpe — often by a wide margin. Pooled across all 80 instrument×timeframe combinations, vol correlates with Sharpe at . That's the opposite of "favor pairs with slightly lower relative volatility."
  • FX as an asset class underperforms crypto, commodities, and equity indices here on every aggregate metric — this rule, as specified, looks like a volatility/momentum harvester more than a low-vol carry-style system.
  • Crypto's daily-timeframe numbers are inflated by the asset simply having risen for a decade. BTCUSD's huge 1d raw return partly reflects being long Bitcoin through a multi-year bull market, not proof the rule adds edge there — no buy-and-hold benchmark is computed here to separate the two.
  • 4h bars are built by resampling 1h bars, aligned to clock hours (00/04/08/12… UTC), not to any particular session open. Still no transaction costs, spread, or funding anywhere in Part 2 — this matters most at 15m/30m, where a realistic spread could erase the edge entirely.
  • Different assets carry different history lengths even within "1d," and results for the same asset across timeframes are not independent trials — treat the 80-combination grid as a wide sweep, not 80 independent experiments.
  • Long only, throughout. Part 3 below mirrors the exact same rule into a short entry below the SMA18 and re-runs the identical grid to see whether adding the short side helps or hurts.

Part 3 · adding the short side

Long + short: mirror the rule below the SMA18 — does it help?

Same 18-period SMA, same touch-exit. Short entry — close below SMA18 on two consecutive candles → sell at the close of the second. Short exit — flat as soon as price touches the SMA18 again from below. Reading Part 3 & the head-to-head honestly

  • Adding the short side made things worse in about 4 of every 5 combinations tested () — over this sample, these markets spent more time trending up than down, so a rule that mirrors the long entry into a short entry mostly means "sell into an uptrend, buy back the countertrend bounce," which is the losing side of momentum more often than not.
  • The one place shorting is roughly neutral-to-helpful is FX — the asset class where price action was the most two-sided over this window. It's still the worst-performing class in absolute terms either way; adding shorts doesn't rescue it, it just stops making it (quite as much) worse.
  • Commodities and crypto lose the most from adding shorts — exactly the asset classes that trended up hardest and most persistently in Part 2. This is consistent, not a coincidence: a system built to ride sustained trends will be hurt most by fading the strongest trends.
  • This is still one historical sample, now longer but not out-of-sample tested. A different multi-year stretch with more two-sided or bearish regimes (a prolonged commodities bear market, a crypto winter) could flip this conclusion for those asset classes specifically — the head-to-head table above is there so you can see exactly which instrument/timeframe combinations would need to reverse for that to happen.
  • Same cost, fill, and sample-size caveats as Part 2 apply here, doubled — a long+short system trades roughly twice as often as long-only on the same instrument, so spread/slippage costs (still not modeled) would bite twice as hard.
